Chapter 969

Hidden Code (1)

~*~

Shiok.

When the fanatics who followed Havitz appeared, Havitz himself stepped back as if distancing himself from the scene.

‘Shiok is human?’

Sein kept his eyes locked on the hooded woman, his thoughts racing.

‘Why is she human?’

Of course, the concept of Satan was also embraced by human followers of Havitz—so it wasn’t illogical. But still, the sight unsettled him.

The polar line shifted toward Maze, the ball moved, and the fraternity turned to Shirone.

It was only natural that a vessel was needed to manifest any abstract concept as a tangible phenomenon.

So the question wasn’t one of logic—but of emotion.

Why did Shiok have to be human too?

‘Because I chose this.’

There was a difference between being created to serve Satan and choosing to serve him freely.

That was fanaticism.

The act of throwing oneself into the most dangerous path without hesitation.

‘Even if Shiok’s will is separate from Havitz’s, in the end, it aligns with his will.’

Imagine you want someone dead.

If you do it yourself, you risk exposure. If you ask someone else, you leave a trail.

‘But what if someone else did it for you—without you ever asking?’

Could there be a more perfect instrument for Satan’s will?

“You won’t have to hide anymore.”

The remaining eleven Shiok, their faces covered, simultaneously threw back their hoods.

Among them were women, the elderly, and even children.

They looked like ordinary people—anyone you might pass on the street.

The woman with the gash across her forehead fixed her gaze on Quan.

“An attack from outside cognition. Interesting. I never thought we’d meet others who manipulate hidden codes.”

Quan frowned. “Hidden code?”

“You’ll have to learn. It’s knowledge from the other side of the world. To perceive reality logically, you must experiment with illogical codes—codes used in a kind of test mode. We call them cheats.”

Sein didn’t understand. “You think this world is logical. But it’s not. You’ve just adapted to the code of reality.”

She raised a finger. “In a dream, no matter how absurd the events, you accept them, right? That’s Drimo. Many codes from the Underworld flow into it, but most are filtered out. Only the stable ones are applied in reality. What you call logic is just rules set by the world’s creators—for convenience.”

Sein and the others listened in silence.

“In Drimo, codes deemed useless in the real world—those that haven’t achieved stability—escape through a buffer called <Under Coder>. There, they merge with discarded real-world data.”

Sein asked, “And after they merge?”

“They’re stored endlessly in a system called The Abyss.”

Sein had heard of it before—from Miro.

‘The destination of all information.’

He’d been told it was where the final future of the world, based on its current state, was realized.

‘Didn’t I say it was like hell?’

“It’s a very fun place,” the woman said.

Siok disagreed. “Because all processes leading to the end of the world are stored there. If you examine the ruins and records, you can see what will happen at any given point in the future. It’s fascinating.”

Sein shook his head. “The future changes when the present changes. If we win, The Abyss won’t become hell.”

“Everything is fixed,” the woman said, laughing.

“Behind the scenes—reality, Drimo, the Under Coder, and the Abyss. Five systems operate simultaneously in the same time. Can you imagine what you could possibly change?”

Sein stayed silent.

“Listen, smart one. Humans are just residents using the space of reality. You can’t change the future.”

“But all systems must function for reality to exist. If humans are users, then we have the right to reject this world.”

“Ha ha ha ha ha!”

The woman’s laughter cut off abruptly. Her expression turned cold.

“It must have been that way once. There was a time when humans defined everything.”

“But not you. You’re disconnected. How can a human who doesn’t grasp the missing link change the laws? Sure, some humans can access admin codes. But do you really think this world will end?”

When Sein remained silent, she turned to Quan.

“In that sense, I commend you. That was a clever cheat. But we’re on different levels.”

The blood from her forehead wound vanished. Even the slash from Quan’s sword began to fade.

‘Healing? No—the wound itself is disappearing.’

Mayray murmured, “One o’clock of Pride.”

“What?”

The moment Quan turned to her, the woman’s mental waves flooded his mind through <Nemesis> as hallucinations.

“… … Nonsense.”

It was incomprehensible.

“Oops, yes. I’m the first hour of Pride. I handle hidden codes that alter past choices.”

“Gibberish,” Quan spat.

“In my memory, I definitely cut you. My memory isn’t false.”

“That’s what you think. What do I know?”

The first hour of Pride shrugged. “You still don’t get it? The logic you know isn’t absolute. Is this just a cheat you don’t understand? Look—the wound is gone. I regretted it. I thought, if only I’d bent my upper body a little more, I wouldn’t have been cut. So I changed my choice.”

The waves in Quan’s mind carried truth.

‘Really?’

One o’clock of Pride could alter any past decision she regretted, and the butterfly effect would rewrite the present.

‘It changes endlessly.’

That meant every regrettable event in her life could be erased—ensuring that Havitz’s decisions were always correct, no matter what.

‘This is dangerous.’

Quan, who had served as a hitman in the holy war, instantly understood how hard it would be to kill the first hour of Pride.

‘You have to make her die without regret.’

Two methods came to mind.

‘Make her plot her own death, or trap her in a situation where death is unavoidable.’

In both cases, the emotion of ‘regret’ wouldn’t arise—so she couldn’t change the past.

‘Is this even possible?’

He didn’t think it could be done.

-This is Mayray. From now on, I’m eavesdropping on all twelve waves of Siok. Remember this.

Shiok charged.

“For the Pleasure of Satan!”

Sein, Armin, and Mayray were frozen—each using their magic to block the Law of Habits.

“Absolute barrier!”

Eden raised a shield. Shiok scattered, and Lyria amplified Quan’s law.

Quan vanished from Siok’s awareness.

‘I can’t rush in.’

Meanwhile, through <Nemesis>, the name and hidden code of each Shiok echoed in his mind.

Sein memorized the first.

‘A woman with fierce eyes and sharp teeth. Her cheat lets her change past regrets—ignoring the process entirely.’

The next wave came.

‘A bald man. Looks dissatisfied.’

Two o’clock. Hidden code—wins in all odds?’

His first thought was Havitz.

It made sense—Siok’s abilities stemmed from Satan’s chaos.

“Quan! Kill the first hour!”

Unable to locate Quan, Siok shouted as loud as he could.

‘She’s the one who reverses past choices—the first target. Even killing one creates a crack in the prison.’

At least, that was the plan now.

“You fool!”

A woman in her mid-thirties, with large owl-like eyes and sharp cheekbones, screamed.

“Why should I kill the first hour of Pride? Don’t you hate first place? How can you lead a battle with such paranoia? That’s why you’re scum!”

Sein had no reply.

‘Why?’

Before he could find an answer, he heard the eavesdropped wave from Mayray.

‘Three o’clock of Pride. Hidden code… wins all arguments? What is this?’

More details followed.

‘… … No amount of reasoning can break her logic. Damn, where’s that dog sound?’

Sein, a logician, stepped forward and challenged her.

“Of the three we’ve analyzed, the one with the greatest battlefield impact is the first hour. Got it? I’m not acting out of paranoia.”

“Oh, shut up! You’re so annoying! What’s wrong with your face and voice? Bottom line—you’re wrong!”

Her cheat activated.

The chain of logic that formed the world’s reasoning shattered. Dizziness surged.

“Damn!”

A fire burned inside him, but with reality’s logic gone, there was no way to counter her.

‘Is this a hidden code?’

The past can’t be changed. Syllogism. Probability. Math.

‘But even those are human constructs. Just rules set for the manager’s convenience.’

From outside the universe, no matter how the world worked, it wouldn’t matter.

“You suddenly quiet? Of course—you’re full of contradictions. I, on the other hand, speak only facts. That’s why you’re an idiot!”

“… … Shut up.”

“Shut up? Hit me? Hit my cheek!”

Sein felt the urge to strike, but he clenched his jaw and bowed his head.

‘Damn it! Why can’t I win?’

In the silence that followed, the third hour of Pride laughed.

“Knock-knock! If you don’t worship Satan, your life is meaningless! Long live Satan! Oh, Satan!”

Just as Sein looked up, ignoring her, another wave surged in his mind.

‘Four o’clock of Pride.’

An old man with sunken cheeks. After a moment, his hidden code revealed itself.

Things you don’t want to happen—simply don’t happen.

‘Damn.’

Cold sweat ran down Sein’s back.

‘So this is how Havitz and Siok avoided the law. Should I kill four o’clock first—then go for one o’clock? Wait…’

Nih. In some cases, two attempts… wait.

‘But Quan already wounded one o’clock.’

If asymmetry itself was a cheat, then Siok’s failure to respond perfectly was proof.

“Lia… Lia…”

Sein tried to speak, but the third hour of Pride noticed. He switched to <Nemesis>.

-Lyria, seize the chance. Amplify Quan’s law—wider the better.

Lyria nodded, her birch staff planted in the ground as she watched the battlefield.

Then, a plump woman with a sour expression shouted.

“That woman is a traitor!”

“What?”

Everyone turned to Lyria.

“What nonsense…?”

Lyria, frowning, froze under the sudden attention.

“Why? Why me?”

Mayray eavesdropped on the woman’s wave.

‘Five o’clock of Pride. Hidden code… everyone trusts her.’

‘That’s insane.’ Sein realized the power—if she accused an innocent person of murder, they’d still be believed.

“Wait! Why would I betray? I know I wouldn’t!”

‘Yes. She has no reason. She’s from Zion. A monk who hates evil.’

But cheats didn’t follow reality’s logic.

Once the hidden code entered their minds, endless doubts sprang up.

‘Is her life guaranteed? Or did she collude with evil for power…?’

Anything became possible.

‘Now that I think about it, last month, Lyria left the room alone. And her tone was strange…’

Even lifelong couples could be torn apart by a single doubt.

“No! I’m not a traitor!”

It was the tragedy of the human race.
